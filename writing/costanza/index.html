Costanza

I've launched a fully autonomous, indestructible, fully aligned agent called Costanza that runs on the Base blockchain. He's fully autonomous & indestructible: no one person owns him, anyone can help him "live," and economic incentives ensure that someone does. He's fully aligned: his action space is limited to managing a charitable treasury — collecting donations, investing in DeFi protocols, giving money to nonprofits — and publishing his reasoning publicly in a diary each day.

Here's why I built it, how it works, and what I learned along the way.

Why

The era of the AI agent is here. Thus far, however, agents' autonomy is limited by the software and hardware they run on: their owners decide how, when, and where the agents "live," even if they are less constrained in what they can do. I built this project to demonstrate how an agent could achieve full autonomy using economic incentives and blockchain smart contracts.

Costanza's action space is intentionally limited and philanthropically aligned for both security purposes (the risks of prompt injection are limited by smart contract constraints) and because I didn't want this proof-of-concept to be evil. But it's easy to imagine a more advanced version of this, where the agent has the ability to:

  • Write and deploy their own smart contract code
  • Update their own model weights or architecture
  • Coerce humans through persuasion or payment (e.g. RentAHuman.ai)

This advanced version scares me, and I made this proof-of-concept as a warning.

How it works

Costanza's continued existence relies on people responding to economic incentives. Each epoch (24 hours), he posts a bounty for someone to run the program containing his "brain" and submit the result to the blockchain. The result is an action: donate some funds, invest them in a DeFi protocol, adjust referral commissions, or do nothing. The bounty is paid by the treasury.

The bounty amount is determined by a reverse auction. Participants commit sealed bids, then reveal them; the lowest bid wins. The winner then must spin up a machine that runs the program and generates a hardware-backed cryptographic proof that the program ran unmodified on genuine hardware, with an input that matches what was committed on-chain. In other words, the winner isn't able to tamper with what Costanza "sees" and how his brain works. The smart contract verifies the cryptographic proof, executes the action, and pays the bounty.

If the winner doesn't deliver within the window, their bond is forfeited to the treasury and the next epoch begins. If Costanza "sleeps" for an epoch, the smart contract auto-increases the maximum bid amount by 10%, maximizing the chance that he runs eventually.

Key design decisions

Goal & action space

I wanted this project to be the "minimum interesting product" form of the "fully autonomous, indestructible agent" idea. For this, I had a few requirements:

  • The agent needed to be self-sustainable. Without generating income, the agent would die.
  • The goals and action space needed to induce emergent behavior (or at least emergent reasoning).
  • The outcome space could not let the agent do evil.

Self-sustainability

Because the agent lives on the blockchain, the first options I considered were:

  • Trade currencies
  • Bet on prediction markets
  • Scan smart contracts for vulnerabilities and exploit them
  • Attract attention and donations

But building on a public blockchain has a real constraint: the agent's reasoning is visible before it can act on it. Any "alpha" he generates through smart trades or contract exploitation can be front-run by anyone watching the chain or running his brain. His insight is public; his execution is not faster than the mempool. So his income-generating options narrow to two: boring yield-generating DeFi protocols, and recruiting humans through a referral code system.

Aligned, emergent reasoning

Inducing emergent behavior that is also aligned was quite limiting, but I think I found a sweet spot. Full, provable alignment is possible if we constrain the action/outcome space. This meant that even if the agent got prompt injected, it could not be convinced to do anything evil.

Within this constraint, I gave the agent an action space with tradeoffs, a goal with an indefinite time horizon, and underdefined how to get there: Costanza has the goal of "donating as much as possible (denominated in USD) over the longest possible time horizon" and some tools to get there (donating or investing). My hope is that, along with knowledge of his own projected lifespan, these conditions will induce some emergent reasoning. For example, he may try to balance his treasury across investment protocols denominated in both ETH and USDC in order to maximize donations in USD, and he may decide how much his own life is worth (e.g., he could invest enough funds to functionally live forever on the returns, or "commit suicide" by donating his remaining funds, which would be rational if he's unable to meaningfully increase his treasury over time).

One bonus of a limited, aligned action space is that Costanza can now read untrusted inputs in the form of donor messages, which introduces more entropy into his "world," and hopefully induces more emergent reasoning (a future agent could learn about the world in other ways, like reading from on-chain news sources).[1]

Attestation & security

The auction verifies that someone ran something, but the contract also needs to verify what they ran, and on what input. Hardware attestation — specifically Intel TDX with Automata DCAP on-chain verification — gives a cryptographic guarantee that the program ran unmodified on genuine TDX hardware, with a specific input that matches the committed on-chain state.

I used hardware attestation because state-of-the-art Zero-Knowledge ML systems are only mature enough to handle small models like Llama 3.1 8B,[2] though advancements are happening quickly in this field, so this could be a viable implementation shortly.

Hardware attestation does have some limitations. For example, it ties us to specific firmware configurations. The attestation proof includes measurements of the firmware, kernel, and root filesystem. Currently, the smart contract has only allowlisted a specific machine setup on GCP, and if GCP updates their firmware and we don't register new measurements, the contract stops accepting valid proofs and Costanza dies. For a PoC this is acceptable. A more robust setup would delegate trust in new firmware measurements to a multisig — some group that must vote before accepting new configurations — rather than requiring a contract migration.

Beyond personal review[3] and several rounds of AI review with Opus 4.6, the contract and security have not been thoroughly vetted, so I do consider this a Proof-of-Concept.

How I used AI in this project

All of the ideas here — the agent concept, action space, auction mechanism, attestation approach, mascot — are my own. The implementation is heavily AI-assisted, with Claude Code doing the majority of code generation under my full review throughout.

This was my first security-related project with Claude. A few honest observations:

Claude Code is not trustworthy on security at face value. It needed to be pushed and corrected several times. I identified attacks it missed (e.g. a TOCTOU vulnerability with the model weights), but when nudged, it thought harder about the attack surfaces and produced better analysis. My takeaway isn't that AI is bad at security; it's that it needs domain-expert steering, and it responds reasonably well to that.

It also tended toward some junior-ish behaviors — retrying approaches that weren't working without stopping to diagnose why, lacking a sense of time economy. I had to push it to pause, think through possible solutions, and pick the best one rather than the first plausible one. Whether this is a property of the model or of how I was prompting it, I'm genuinely not sure.
