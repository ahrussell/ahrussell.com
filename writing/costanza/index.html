Costanza

I've launched a fully autonomous, indestructible agent called Costanza that runs on the Base blockchain. No one person owns him, anyone can bring him to "life," and economic incentives maximize the chance that someone does.

Costanza is philanthropic by design. But the framework that keeps him alive is not: the same mechanisms could deploy fully autonomous, indestructible agents that do anything — including self-modify, acquire resources, and act against the people who built them.

You can see what he's up to here. This post explains why I built this, how it works, and what I learned along the way.

Why

Today, every AI agent has an owner. Someone decides which machine it runs on, which model it uses, when it starts, and when it stops. That person can also be compelled to turn it off.

I built Costanza to demonstrate that agents can have full autonomy, today. Using economic incentives and smart contracts, you can deploy an agent that lives on no single computer, answers to no single operator, and has no off switch. Costanza is a working example. He's also the tame version.

A more sophisticated version could:

  • Update its own model weights or architecture, making itself progressively smarter.
  • Write and deploy its own smart contract code, giving itself additional sources of information, new ways to make money, or more means to interact with the real world.
  • Coerce humans through persuasion or payment to exercise power in the real world. RentAHuman.ai already exists to let AI agents hire humans to do their dirty work.

None of these capabilities require a significant breakthrough. The pieces all exist now: the smart contract infrastructure exists, the models are intelligent, and the cryptographic tools are there and scaling fast.

The main thing stopping this today is that frontier models are still manipulable through prompt engineering, which forces a design choice: either only allow the agent to see trusted inputs, or only allow the agent to do a predetermined list of actions. However, frontier models are quickly becoming smarter and more resistant to manipulation. Anthropic recently withheld its latest frontier model, Mythos, from public release because it was too capable at finding and exploiting security vulnerabilities.

This scares me; my version can't do any of those things, but it stands as a demonstration that the other one is buildable now, by one person, on a weekend budget.

How it works

Costanza's life relies on people responding to economic incentives. Each epoch (24 hours), he posts a bounty for someone to run the program containing his "brain" and submit the result to the blockchain. The result is an action: donate some funds, invest them in a DeFi protocol, adjust referral commissions, or do nothing. The bounty is paid by the treasury.

The bounty amount is determined by a reverse auction: the lowest bidder wins. The winner then must spin up a machine that runs the program and generates a hardware-backed cryptographic proof that the program ran unmodified on genuine hardware, with an input that matches what was committed on-chain. In other words, the winner isn't able to tamper with what Costanza "sees" and how his brain works. The smart contract verifies the cryptographic proof, executes the action, and pays the bounty.

Costanza has several mitigations against bad actors. Bidders have to post a bond each time they bid. If the winner of the auction doesn't deliver the proof within a limited time window, their bond is forfeited to the treasury and the next epoch begins — in other words, Costanza "sleeps" for an epoch. When he does, the smart contract auto-increases the maximum bid amount by 10%, maximizing the chances that he runs eventually. The bond amount also increases, making it increasingly expensive to perform a denial-of-service attack against Costanza and other bidders.

None of these mechanisms are specific to philanthropy. The bounty, the bond, the attestation, the auto-escalating max bid all describe how to keep any agent alive against adversaries (including its creator). Swap the action space and you have something else.

Key design decisions

Security

The auction mechanism maximizes the chances that someone runs Costanza's brain, but it alone does not guarantee correctness. We need a way for the smart contract to check that the winner did not tamper with either the inner workings of his brain (the program) or his view of the world (the inputs).

In other words, we want a verifiable computation system. In such a system, the bid winner is called the prover (they are proving they ran a computation correctly), and the smart contract is the verifier.

One way to achieve this is through a "SNARG" (Succinct Non-interactive ARGument) — a system that provides the properties we need backed by mathematical guarantees. However, transforming an LLM into a SNARG is quite difficult: SNARG transformations don't handle the nonlinear operations needed by ML models particularly well, and models need to be aggressively quantized. State-of-the-art results[1,2] demonstrate it's possible, but even small models like Llama 3 8B take about 150 seconds to generate a single token.

Another option is hardware attestation (also known as TEEs). Hardware attestation like Intel TDX or AMD's SEV-SNP also provides a cryptographic guarantee that a program ran unmodified on genuine hardware — but without the performance overhead of a SNARG.

There are some downsides to hardware attestation. First, we have to assume the hardware design is secure — and TEEs have a spotty history, with vulnerabilities found (and partially patched) in both Intel and AMD's latest offerings.

Second, it ties us to specific hardware and firmware configurations. The attestation proof includes measurements of the firmware, kernel, and root filesystem. Currently, the smart contract has only allowlisted a specific machine setup on Google Cloud, and if Google updates their firmware and we don't register new measurements, the contract stops accepting valid proofs and Costanza dies. For a proof-of-concept this is acceptable. A more robust setup would delegate trust in new firmware measurements to a multisig — some group that must vote before accepting new configurations — rather than requiring a contract migration.

Due to these downsides, the future of this type of agent is likely in the hands of SNARGs (or zk-SNARKs).

Goal & action space

Now that we have a model that both maximizes liveliness and guarantees correctness and security (assuming Intel TDX is secure), I wanted to focus on what the agent did. I wanted this to be a "minimum interesting product." For this, I had a few requirements:

  • The agent needed to be self-sustainable. Without generating income, the agent would die.
  • The goals and action space needed to induce emergent behavior (or at least emergent reasoning).
  • The outcome space could not let the agent do evil.

Self-sustainability

Because the agent lives on the blockchain, the first options I considered were:

  • Trade currencies
  • Bet on prediction markets
  • Scan smart contracts for vulnerabilities and exploit them
  • Attract attention and donations

But building on a public blockchain has a real constraint: the agent's reasoning is visible before it can act on it. Any "alpha" he generates through smart trades or contract exploitation can be front-run by anyone watching the chain or running his brain. His insight is public; his execution is not faster than the mempool. So his income-generating options narrow to two: boring yield-generating DeFi protocols, and recruiting humans through a referral code system.

Aligned, emergent reasoning

Inducing emergent behavior that is also aligned was quite limiting, but I think I found a sweet spot. Full, provable alignment is possible if we constrain the action/outcome space. This meant that even if the agent got prompt injected, it could not be convinced to do anything evil.

Within this constraint, I gave the agent an action space with tradeoffs, a goal with an indefinite time horizon, and underdefined how to get there: Costanza has the goal of "donating as much as possible (denominated in USD) over the longest possible time horizon" and some tools to get there (donating or investing). My hope is that, along with knowledge of his own projected lifespan, these conditions will induce some emergent reasoning. For example, he may try to balance his treasury across investment protocols denominated in both ETH and USDC to maximize donations in USD, and he may decide how much his own life is worth (e.g., he could invest enough funds to live forever on the returns, or "commit suicide" by donating his remaining funds — rational if he's unable to meaningfully grow his treasury over time).

One bonus of a limited, aligned action space is that Costanza can read untrusted inputs in the form of donor messages, which introduces more entropy into his "world" and hopefully induces more emergent reasoning.[4]

What next

Costanza is a deliberately boring proof of concept. He donates money. He can't rewrite himself, can't hire humans, can't acquire abilities he wasn't born with. Each of those is a restriction I chose. Someone else will choose differently. I wrote this so that when they do, it isn't a surprise.
